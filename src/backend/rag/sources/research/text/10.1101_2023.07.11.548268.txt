Polygenic architecture of flowering time and its relationship with local environments in the grass Brachypodium distachyon.

Synchronizing the timing of reproduction with the environment is crucial in the wild. Among the multiple mechanisms annual plants evolved to sense their environment, the requirement of cold-mediated vernalization is a major process that prevents individuals from flowering during winter. In many annual plants including crops, both a long and short vernalization requirement can be observed within species, resulting in so-called early-(spring) and late (winter)-flowering genotypes. Here, using the grass model Brachypodium distachyon, we explored the link between flowering time-related trait (vernalization requirement and flowering time), environmental variation, and diversity at flowering-time genes by combining measurements under greenhouse and outdoor conditions. These experiments confirmed that B. distachyon natural accessions display large differences regarding vernalization requirements and ultimately flowering time. We underline significant, albeit quantitative effects of current environmental conditions on flowering time-related traits. While disentangling the confounding effects of population structure on flowering time-related traits remains challenging, population genomics analyses indicate that well-characterized flowering-time genes may contribute significantly to flowering time variation and display signs of polygenic selection. Flowering-time genes, however, do not colocalize with GWAs peaks obtained with outdoor measurements, suggesting that additional genetic factors contribute to flowering time variation in the wild. Altogether, our study fosters our understanding of the polygenic architecture of flowering time in a natural grass system and opens new avenues of research to investigate the gene-by-environment interaction at play for this trait.

Introduction  46 
The induction of reproduction is a critical fitness -related trait in the wild (Gaudinier 47 
and Blackman 2020), as a failure to produce offspring during the growing season may 48 
lead to  the extinction of the individual’s genotype . In many annual plant species 49 
adapted to temperate climates, plantlets establish themselves in the fall and overwin-50 
ter before flowering and producing seeds in more favorable spring conditions 51 
(Chouard 1960; Blackman 2017). Vernalization, the prolonged exposure to cold neces-52 
sary to render plants competent to flower (Chouard 1960), is hence a key component 53 
of plant reproduction as it prevents individuals from flowering prior to winter. The 54 
adaptive potential and the genetic architecture of flowering time has been studied in 55 
an unrivaled manner in Arabidopsis thaliana (for review Andrés and Coupland 2012; 56 
Blümel et al. 2015; Takou et al. 2019) due to the broad geographical distribution of the 57 
species and large genomic resources developed by the community (but see Hall et al. 58 
2006; Monnahan and Kelly 2017; Yan et al. 2021  for works on other Brassicaceae and 59 
Mimulus guttatus). Vernalization is yet controlled by different genes in different plant 60 
groups and likely evolved independently multiple times during flowering plant diver-61 
sification (Ream et al. 2012; Bouché et al., 2017; Raissig and Woods 2022) . Moreover, 62 
specific crop flowering-time genes (e.g. ID1 in maize) are for instance lacking homo-63 
logs in A. thaliana (Blümel, Dally, and Jung 2015). As a wild monocot, the model for the 64 
temperate grasses Brachypodium distachyon constitute a prime system to study the  65 
evolution of flowering-time genes. In this context, we made use of the diversity panel 66 
developed for this species (Gordon et al. 2017; Gordon et al. 2020; Skalska et al. 2020; 67

B. distachyon accessions display large phenotypic variation with regard to flowering 90 
time (e.g. Ream et al. 2014; Gordon et al. 2017; Woods et al., 2019; Sharma et al. 2017), 91 
with some accessions requiring little to no vernalization to flower rapidly (early flow-92 
ering accessions) in certain photoperiods, while other accessions require a few weeks 93 
to several months of vernalization in order to flower (late flowering accessions) (Ream 94 
et al., 2014; Gordon et al., 2017; Woods et al., 2019). These flowering differences have 95 
been described as potentially adaptive and responsible for population distribution ac-96 
cording to climate variation (Gordon et al. 2017; Woods et al. 2019; Skalska et al. 2020). 97 
However, the extent to which variation in vernalization requirement and ultimately 98 
flowering time correlates with local environmental conditions has yet to be formally 99 
tested in this species. For instance, whether late -flowering genotypes, which require 100 
long vernalization treatments, have been selected to complete their life cycle at a 101 
slower rate to overcome harsher or longer winter, as observed in Swedish populations 102 
of A. thaliana (Ågren et al. 2017), remains an open question.  103 
In a Mediterranean species like B. distachyon, not all accessions might be exposed 104 
to harsh winter and short cold periods might be sufficient to allow permissive flowering 105 
in the wild. In this study, we hence decomposed flowering time into  four flowering 106 
time-related traits: the minimum threshold duration (MTD) of vernalization necessary 107 
to be permissive for flowering, the number of day to flower when MTD is received, the 108 
saturating threshold duration of vernalization above which no further reduction in 109 
flowering time is gained and the fastest time for a given accession to flower from the 110 
day plants after vernalization saturation. W e combined measurements under 111

Flowering time -related trait  measurement s 134 
We performed an experiment in controled conditions from October 2021 until May 135 
2022 in order to test the flowering phenology of the five genetic clades of 136 
Brachypodium distachyon. Twelve accessions per genetic clade were selected, in addi-137 
tion to the reference accession Bd21. All accessions were treated with five vernalization 138 
periods spanning from 2 to 10 weeks, with three replicates per treatment and per 139 
accession. Seeds were stratified for at least two weeks before the experiment, and then 140 
sowed in pots that were placed in greenhouse conditions (16 h day at 20°C and 8 h 141 
dark at 18°C with a light intensity of 200 µMol/m2/s ). We distributed the replicates 142 
randomly across trays  to minimize bias due to position effects. Three weeks after 143 
germination the plants were transfered to a cooling chamber (constant temperature 144 
at 4°C, 8h light 80 µMol/m2/s, and 16 h dark) for 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 weeks. At the end 145 
of the vernalization treatment, plants were moved back to the greenhouse. 146 
Flowering time was measured as the number of days after return to greenhouse to 147 
the first day of spike emergence which is consistent with stage 50 of the Zadoks scale 148 
that was used in (Ream et al. 2014). Measurements were taken every two days until the 149 
end of the experiment in May.  During the experiments, trays were permuted on the 150 
table to further limit position effects.  151 
We then extracted from this experiment four flowering time -related traits: i)  the 152 
minimum threshold duration of vernalization necessary to be permissive for flowering, 153 
ii) the number of day to flower when the minimum threshold is received, iii) the satu-154 
rating threshold duration of vernalization above which no further reduction in 155

Redundancy analysis (RDA)  178 
We extracted the 19 classical bioclimatic variables as well as elevation, aridity and solar 179 
radiation in spring for the 56 accessions for which flowering time -related trait were 180 
measured. We performed a principal component analysis (PCA) with the R base func-181 
tion prcomp using the resulting 22 variables.  182 
Association between flowering time-related traits, bioclimatic variables and genetic 183 
cluster of origin were tested with redundancy analysis (RDA) using the R package Ve-184 
gan 2.6-4 (O'Hara et al. 2022) following (Capblancq and Forester 2023). RDA computes 185 
axes that are linear combinations of the explanatory variables. In other words, this 186 
method seeks, in successive order, a series of linear combinations of the explanatory 187 
variables that best explain the variation of the response matrix (Borcard, Gillet and 188 
Legendre 2018). It is therefore especially suited when explanatory variables are corre-189 
lated. 190 
To identify which variable influence flowering time -related traits, we opted for a 191 
forward selection approach (Capblancq and Forester 2023) and first ran empty models 192 
where the respective flowering time-related trait were treated independently as a re-193 
sponse variable and explained by a fixed intercept alone. We then ran additional mod-194 
els where each flowering time-related traits were treated independently as a response 195 
variable while the cluster of origin, the 22 environmental variables mentioned above  196 
or both the cluster of origin and the 22 environmental variables were successively en-197 
tered as explanatory variables. These full models were compared to their respective 198

Scans of selection  247 
The XTX analysis was performed with BayPass v2.3 using the five genetic clades or three 248 
genetic lineages as populations (Gautier 2015). We generated the input file by using 249 
vcftools --count to calculate the allele frequency of each SNP present in our vcf (no 250 
filtering on minor minimum allele frequency). We then ran Baypass on our actual da-251 
taset with the following parameters: -pilotlength 500 -npilot 15 -burnin 2500 -nthreads 252 
6. To calibrate the XTX and define a threshold of significance for differentiated SNPs, 253 
we then used the simulate.baypass function from baypass_utils (Gautier et al. 2015) to 254 
generate a pseudo-observed dataset (POD) of 100,000 loci based on the co -variance 255 
matrix computed with our real dataset. We then ran Baypass on the POD with the 256 
parameters described above. We used the 0.99 quantile of the XTX calculated for the 257 
POD as a threshold of significance for the real dataset. Integrated Haplotype Scores 258

Results  308 
Flowering time measurements under greenhouse conditions  309 
We selected 61 accessions (Figure 1A) from the B. distachyon diversity panel (Minada-310 
kis et al. 2023) for our flowering time experiment. Those accessions were chosen to 311 
represent all five genetic clades and occur, when possible, along latitudinal gradients. 312 
Briefly, we submitted plants to five vernalization treatments (2, 4, 6, 8 or 10 weeks at 313 
4°C) with three replicates each and measured how long plants took to flower after the 314 
return to warm conditions (Figure S1, Table S1 for the raw data). For five out of the 61 315 
accessions (Veg12 from A_East; Ren4, Lb1, Lb23 and Cm7 from A_Italia), none of the 316 
replicates flowered by the end of the experiment (Figure 1a, Figure S1) despite normal 317 
growth. All subsequent analyses were hence performed on the 56 accessions for which 318 
flowering time data were collected.  319 
However, B. distachyon accessions span a large range of habitats (Minadakis et al. 320 
2023). While some accessions of the diversity panel can experience up to -11 °C as a 321

Discussion  639 
Flowering time -related traits and adaptation to local climate  640 
Flowering time has been shown to play an important role in local adaptation across 641 
many plant systems including crops (Izawa 2007; Anderson et al. 2012; Anderson, et 642 
al. 2013; Ågren et al. 2017; Navarro et al. 2017; Wadgymar et al. 2017; Takou et al. 2019; 643 
Qian et al. 2020; Yan et al. 2021) . In B. distachyon , flowering time has been mainly 644 
studied from a molecular viewpoint, clarifying the role of specific genes in sensing 645 
environmental variation such as day -length and temperature ( Ream et al. 2012; 646 
Ruelens et al. 2013; Ream et al. 2014; Woods et al. 2014; Woods et al. 2017 a,b; Lomax 647 
et al. 2018; Cao et al. 2020; Kennedy and Geuten 2020; Woods et al. 2020; Bouché et 648 
al. 2022; Raissig and Woods 2022). While those studies underline the complex archi-649 
tecture of flowering-time and revealed large variation regarding vernalization require-650 
ment among natural accessions for instance, they provide little information with regard 651 
to adaptation.  652 
In fact, t he wide range of vernalization requirement observed in B. distachyon 653 
suggests that local adaptation of flowering time could take place through distinct de-654 
velopmental thresholds (i.e the accumulated amount of ‘developmental time’ required 655 
for a given developmental stage to allow a transition to the next developmental stage 656 
see Donohue et al. 2014 ) among accessions. Indeed, in a widespread Mediterranean 657 
species like B. distachyon, not all accessions are exposed to long and harsh winter (Fig-658 
ure S2). The expression of traits such as the minimum duration of vernalization neces-659 
sary to permit flowering might be contributing in fine to flowering time variation in 660

Early- and late -flowering genotypes are unlikely driven by adaptation  683 
The greenhouse experiment further confirms that B. distachyon natural accessions 684 
vary greatly regarding their vernalization saturation requirement and ultimately flow-685 
ering time and can be broadly grouped into early- and late-flowering genotypes (Fig-686 
ure S1 and Figure 1, but see Ream et al., 2014; Gordon, et al. 2017  for a finer classifi-687 
cation scheme). Despite within clade variation regarding flowering time after vernali-688 
zation saturation (Figure 1c), accessions from the A lineage (A_East and A_Italia) display 689 
a large delay in flowering compared to accessions from the B (B_East and B_West) and 690 
C lineage, which typically result from longer vernalization saturation requirements. This 691 
pattern has been suggested to be a sign of adaptation at a regional scale in Turkey 692 
(Skalska et al. 2020) and could be interpreted as a footprint of diversifying selection at 693 
the genetic clade level. Based on this hypothesis, accessions from the A_East and A_Ita-694 
lia clades may display a delay in flowering as globally adapted to colder and less arid 695 
environmental conditions, as shown for instance in A. thaliana (Ågren and Schemske 696 
2012). Yet, our results do not fully support this scenario, as we only find partial corre-697 
lations between the partitioning of the phenotypes and the bioclimatic variables asso-698 
ciated with flowering time (Figure 1 and Figure S 3). Our niche modelling projections 699 
for the last glacial maximum (Minadakis et al. 2023), do not further support an adap-700 
tation to the last glacial maximum conditions (Figure S 8). As such, the partitioning of 701 
the early vs. late flowering accessions remains difficult to explain with an “adaptive” 702 
scenario. In summary, we conclude that local environmental conditions are partly 703

Conclusion and perspectives  828 
Our results suggest that i) flowering time -related traits are a locally adapted trait in 829 
B. distachyon but ii) part of the variation in those traits  can be explained by the 830 
environment and by SN Ps in key flowering -time genes and, iii) key flowering -time 831 
genes are co-evolving but their effect in the wild remains to be clarified. In the face of 832 
global warming, plant phenology has recently advanced significantly (e.g. Anderson et 833
