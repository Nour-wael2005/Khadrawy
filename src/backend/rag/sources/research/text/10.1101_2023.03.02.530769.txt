Genome biology and evolution of mating type loci in four cereal rust fungi

Obligate heterozygous loci such as sex- or mating-compatibility regions often display suppression of recombination and signals of genomic degeneration. In Basidiomycota, two distinct gene loci confer mating compatibility. These encode for homeodomain (HD) transcription factors and pheromone receptor (Pra)-ligand pairs. To date genome level mating type (MAT) loci analysis is lacking for obligate biotrophic basidiomycetes in the order Pucciniales, which contains many economically important plant pathogens.

Here, we focus on four Puccinia cereal rust species, including P. coronata f. sp. avenae, Puccinia graminis f. sp. tritici, P. triticina and P. striiformis f. sp. tritici, which infect oat and wheat. MAT loci are located on two separate chromosomes supporting previous hypotheses of tetrapolar mating types in the Pucciniales. The HD locus is multiallelic in all four species while the PR locus appears to be biallelic except for P. graminis f. sp. tritici which displays genetic features of more than two alleles. HD loci were largely conserved in their macrosynteny within and between species without strong signals of recombination suppression. PR loci proximate regions, however, displayed extensive signs of recombination suppression and genomic degeneration in the three species with a clear biallelic PR locus. These observations suggest a correlation between recombination suppression, genomic degeneration and allele status of MAT loci which is consistent with recent mathematical modelling and simulations. Finally, we confirm the evolutionary conservation of MAT gene expression during the asexual infection cycle of the cereal host which we propose is related to correct nuclear pairing during spore formation. Together, our study provides insights into the evolution of MAT loci of key pathogenic Puccinia species. This detailed understanding is important to predict possible combinations of nuclear pairs that can arise via sexual reproduction or somatic recombination to enable the evolution of newly virulent isolates of these important plant pathogens.

Author summarySex of animals and some plants is determined by sex chromosomes. In fungi, mate compatibility is determined by mating type (MAT) loci, which share some features with sex chromosomes including recombination suppression around heterozygous loci. Here, we study the MAT loci in fungal pathogens from the order Pucciniales that can cause rust diseases on many economically important plants including wheat and oat. We show that one of the MAT loci is multiallelic, while the other is biallelic. The biallelic locus shows strong signs of recombination suppression and genetic deterioration with an increase in transposable elements and gene deserts surrounding the locus. Our findings on the genome biology of MAT loci in four economically important pathogens will lead to a better understanding and prediction of evolution of novel virulent isolates that can lead to large scale pandemics in agriculture.

Introduction 63 
The evolution of ‘sex and mating-type’ determining loci and chromosomes is a fundamental 64 
question in biology. It is widely accepted that X and Y chromosomes were originally 65 
homologous, but recombination suppression gradually resulted in genetic degeneration of the 66 
Y chromosome (1). Genetic degeneration footprints are alleviated rates of (non-)synonymous 67 
substitutions (d N, d S), accumulation of transposable elements (TEs), accumulation of 68

consequence of recombination cessation (2-5). In contrast to many animals and plants, 70 
antagonistic selection is unlikely to be the evolutionary mechanism that causes recombination 71 
cessation in fungal mating-type ( MAT) loci (6-8). Instead, mathematical modelling and 72 
stochastic simulations suggest that recombination suppressors, e.g. inversions or TEs, which 73 
are loaded with less deleterious mutations than average initially, are more likely to be fixed at 74 
permanently heterozygous MAT loci when compared to other autosomal loci. This initial 75 
fixation of recombination suppressors at MAT loci can lead to a progressive extension of non-76 
recombining regions via the accumulation of additional inversion or TEs over longer 77 
evolutionary timeframes (2). Importantly, this model predicts non-recombining regions 78 
around MAT loci are larger at biallelic loci and in species with smaller effective population 79 
size, short haploid phase, outcrossing mating systems, high mutation rates, and extended 80 
dikaryotic life stages. Indeed, several fungal species carry extensive non-recombining regions 81 
around MAT loci. Recombination suppression can range from hundreds of kbp (kilo base pairs) 82 
to several mbp (mega base pairs) and lead to genetic degeneration (5, 9), such as in 83 
Neurospora tetrasperma (10), Podospora anserina (11), Schizothecium tetrasporum (12) and 84 
several Microbotryum spp. (8, 13).  85 
Detailed analyses of MAT loci and their genome biology has been lacking behind for rust 86 
fungi of the order Pucciniales in the division Basidiomycota (9). This is despite the fact that 87 
Pucciniales is the fungal order with the most known plant pathogens with close to 8,000 88 
described species that cause diseases with significant environmental and economic impact for 89 
various trees and crops such as poplar, paperbarks, wheat, oat, soybean, and coffee (14-16). 90 
Overall, Basidiomycota have evolved unique mating systems to govern nuclear compatibility, 91 
mate selection, and completion of the full life cycle (9). From a genetic perspective, two MAT 92 
loci determine mating-type identity and non-self-recognition in most Basidiomycota. The 93

mating pheromone peptide precursor gene ( mfa) (17). The PR  locus defines pre-mating 95 
compatibility and gamete fusion. The PRA protein is a transmembrane localized G protein-96 
coupled receptor, which recognizes the processed and posttranslationally modified mature 97 
pheromone peptide MFA encoded by a matching allelic PR locus (9). Downstream of initial 98 
gamete fusion, the homeodomain ( HD) locus determines success in postmating development 99 
(9). The HD locus contains two tightly linked homeodomain transcription factors genes, (bW-100 
HD1 and bE-HD2 in Pucciniales (18, 19), also known as bE-HD1 and bW-HD2 in other fungi 101

initiate a transcriptional cascade downstream of PRA-MFA activation (9). The HD 104

dikaryotic state, and control pathogenicity in some plant pathogens such as smuts (13). The 106 
HD and PR  loci can be either physically linked or unlinked which means they segregate 107 
together or independently, respectively. The mating system in Basidiomycota influences the 108 
genomic organization and resulting segregation patterns of MAT loci. Most basidiomycete 109 
species are heterothallic, where sexual gametes must carry different alleles at both MAT loci 110 
and intra-haploid mating is not possible. Hence, heterothallic species can either have bipolar 111 
or tetrapolar mating systems. In bipolar species the HD and PR loci are physically linked or 112 
one locus has lost its function in mating. In any case, in bipolar species only one MAT locus 113 
determines mate compatibility. In tetrapolar species, the HD and PR  loci are unlinked and 114 
both determine mate compatibility. Bipolar mating systems are favorable in inbreeding 115 
populations that undergo selfing. Under selfing a single locus is advantageous as the 116 
likelihood of compatibility amongst gametes derived from the same diploid individual is 50% 117 
(13). In comparison, a tetrapolar mating system has at most a 25% compatibility under the 118

Studying mating systems in fungal species of the order Pucciniales has been difficult because 133 
of their obligate biotrophy and often complex life cycles that prevent culturing outside of their 134 
hosts and often requires multiple hosts to complete their sexual life cycle (9). For example, 135 
macrocyclic and heteroecious rust fungi have five spore stages and require two hosts to 136 
complete their asexual and sexual life cycles. During the asexual infection cycle on the 137 
“primary” host, e.g. wheat and oat, rust fungi are dikaryotic and produce re-infective 138 
urediniospores that harbor two distinct nuclei carrying compatible mating types. The sexual 139 
cycle is initiated with the production of teliospores, which represent the short diploid phase of 140 
rust fungi. Resulting haploid basidiospores infect the “alternate” host, e. g. Berberis sp., and 141 
form haploid infection structures with distinct mating types. Fusion of cell  with compatible 142 
mating types generates dikaryotic intercellular mycelia within the plant tissue that gives rise 143

assemblies of rust fungi were fragmented and the structure of the MAT loci could not be 185

datasets. The study species include the oat crown rust fungus P. coronata f. sp.  avenae (38-192

contrast, the AU-test for Pgt suggested similar tree topologies for bW-HD1 and bE-HD2 , 304 
which likely is influenced by the low sample number of four alleles.  305

Discussion 556 
Our comparative analysis of predicted MAT genes in four cereal rust fungi provides novel 557 
insight into the evolution of these genes and their proximal regions within and between 558 
species.  559 
First, we confirmed that HD and PR loci are unlinked and located on two distinct 560 
chromosomes in all four cereal rust fungi, which confirms previous analysis on more 561 
fragmented genome assemblies or for individual rust fungal species (18, 21, 30). Further, both 562 
loci were heterozygous in all twelve dikaryotic genome assemblies. Taken together, these 563 
observations strongly support the hypothesis of tetrapolar mating types in rust fungi. Though 564 
this genome biology informed hypothesis requires experimental validation via crosses and 565 
genetic analysis of resulting offspring. As previously observed for Pt (18), the Pra homologs 566 
STE3.2-1 were highly conserved in all cereal rust fungi and had no mfa genes closely 567 
associated. This suggests that STE3.2-1 does not regulate mate compatibility.  568 
 In P. coronata f. sp.  avenae , P. striiformis f. sp. graminis  and P. striiformis f. sp. tritici, 569 
STE3.2-1 is flanked by anaphase promoting complex APC10/Doc1 which regulates important 570 
events in mitosis (75) and Elongation factor EF1B (76). Together, these findings suggest that 571 
STE3.2-1 might play an important role during the cell cycle or different developmental 572 
programs as reported for other basidiomycete fungi (77, 78).  However as previously reported 573 
for P. triticina (18), expression for STE3.2-1 was absent or very low during the infection 574 
cycle of the cereal host.  575

the two MAT loci. Multiallelism is beneficial under outcrossing conditions, because it 577 
increases the compatibility between randomly selected mates. At the same time, outcrossing 578 
selects for new and rare alleles because these are compatible with existing and more common 579 
alleles (9). For example, U. maydis has many distinct transcription factor alleles at the HD 580 
locus (79, 80). We performed extensive analysis of HD loci extracted from dikaryotic genome 581 
assemblies and developed a workflow that permitted us to de novo reconstruct loci directly 582 
from Illumina short read sequencing data. This enabled us to investigate the allele status of 583 
bW-HD1 and bE-HD2 in 12-15 additional isolates for each cereal rust fungal species without 584 
the need for long-read genome assemblies or targeted amplicon sequencing. In addition, the 585 
analyzed isolates were selected based on them being the best available representation of 586 
global rust fungal lineages without repeated sampling within clonal lineages to facilitate a 587 
more in-depth analysis of the allele status of MAT loci. This extensive analysis demonstrated 588 
that the HD locus is also multiallelic in the four cereal rust fungal species. The four species 589 
had between six to twelve alleles of bW-HD1 and bE-HD2. This supports previous analyses 590 
of the allele status of these genes in P. striiformis f. sp. tritici, which identified nine alleles of 591

underestimate giving limited public datasets. The majority of analyzed isolates were retrieved 593 
from the cereal host during the asexual reproduction cycle and from regions that are known to 594 
be deprived of sexually recombining populations. The only exception was P. coronata f. sp.  595 
avenae isolates, which are reported to be derived from sexual recombining populations in the 596 
United States of America (40). Indeed, we identified the highest allelic variation of HD genes 597 
(eleven bW-HD1 and twelve bE-HD2) in P. coronata f. sp. avenae. This is similar to previous 598

The functions of MAT genes in rust fungi are currently unknown, beyond their likely role in 674 
mediating mate compatibility. However, in P. triticina and M. larici-populina (35, 85), MAT 675 
genes are expressed during the sexual and asexual infection cycle. Suppression of MAT gene 676 
expression in P. triticina  during the asexual infection cycle on wheat reduces infection 677 
severity and spore production (18). Here, we show that MAT genes are also upregulated 678 
during the late stage of the asexual infection cycle of the cereal host in P. coronata f. sp.  679 
avenae, P. graminis f. sp. tritici,  and P. striiformis f. sp. tritici. This suggests a broader 680 
conservation of MAT gene expression during the late stages of the asexual infection cycle of 681 
rust fungi. Rust fungal cells carry more than two nuclei in the same cytosol during the asexual 682 
infection cycle (86). At the same time, ureodiniospores always carry two nuclei of compatible 683 
mating types and never two nuclei of identical mating types. Studies from the early 20 th 684 
century show that the two nuclei of ureodiniospore mother cells undergo synchronous nuclear 685 
division within the same cytoplasm giving rise to four nuclei within the same ureodiniospore 686 
precursor cells. This is followed by nuclear movement to restore appropriate nuclear pairing 687 
and planar cell division that enables ureodiniospore maturation and reconstitutes the 688 
dikaryotic state (86). The observed upregulation of MAT genes aligns with the development of 689 
ureodiniospore primordia development and spore production. Hence, we hypothesize that 690 
MAT genes are involved in the appropriate nuclear pairing at this stage of fungal development.  691 
Understanding mating types in cereal rust fungi has also significant implications for 692 
agriculture and for the prediction of the evolution of new virulent isolates. Recent reports 693 
suggest that nuclear exchange between isolates during the asexual infection cycle (also known 694 
as somatic hybridization (87)), in addition to sexual reproduction, can lead to nuclear 695 
assortment (87). Nuclear exchange and the viability of the resulting offspring is likely 696 
regulated by MAT genes because all proposed events have given rise to isolates with 697 
dikaryotic genomes that carry opposing MAT gene pairs. We confirmed these observations 698
