Soil respiration is correlated with rainfall and soil moisture at multiple temporal scales in a seasonal wet tropical forest.

Soil respiration is the second largest natural flux of carbon (C) between terrestrial ecosystems and the atmosphere, with tropical forests amongst the largest contributors of such soil-derived carbon efflux. With climate change expected to drive changes in both temperature and rainfall, our ability to predict responses of the carbon cycle in the future hinges upon an understanding of how these factors influence soil respiration (Rs). However, these relationships remain poorly characterised across the seasonal tropics, especially south Asia. Here, for two seasonal tropical sites in the Western Ghats of India, we characterised annual, seasonal and temporal variation in Rs and assessed its temperature and moisture sensitivity over 6 years. At both sites, Rs was positively correlated with temperature at the instantaneous scale, but showed no relationship with temperature at seasonal or annual scales. In contrast, Rs showed significant relationships with rainfall and soil moisture at all temporal scales. At the annual scale, respiration was negatively correlated with total annual rainfall. At the seasonal scale, wet season Rs was significantly lower than in the dry season. At the instantaneous scale, Rs showed a parabolic relationship with soil moisture, where soil respiration increased with soil moisture up to [~]21% and decreased beyond that point. Our results suggest that future responses of soil respiration in this seasonal tropical forest will largely be driven by changes in the Indian Summer Monsoon, not only mean annual precipitation, but also the frequency and intensity of extreme rainfall events.

1. Introduction:  31 
Tropical forests store about 55% of the global terrestrial carbon, of which nearly a third (~32%) 32 
is sequestered in soils (Pan et al., 2011). Given that soils are so carbon rich, the release of carbon 33 
from soils is of much importance, and can play critical roles in regulating atmospheric CO2 34 
concentrations and regional and global climate. Soil respiration (Rs) - comprising both 35 
autotrophic (root), and heterotrophic (bacterial and mycorrhizal) respiration - is a major 36 
contributor to the land-atmosphere carbon flux, releasing ~75-98 Pg y-1 of C back to the 37 
atmosphere globally (Bond-Lamberty and Thomson, 2010a; Raich and Potter, 1995), with 38 
tropical forests amongst the largest contributors of these soil derived atmospheric carbon fluxes 39 
(Raich and Schlesinger, 1992; Warner et al., 2019). 40 
Rates of soil respiration (Rs) are governed by complex interactions between a range of biotic and 41 
abiotic drivers, of which climatic factors such as temperature and moisture availability are 42 
particularly important (Bond-Lamberty and Thomson, 2010a; Hursh et al., 2017; Lei et al., 2021; 43 
Raich and Tufekciogul, 2000). Future projected changes in both temperature and rainfall patterns 44 
are thus likely to have significant impacts on soil derived land-atmosphere fluxes of CO2, with 45 
potential feedbacks on regional and global climates. Although temperature explains much of the 46 
variability in Rs across different biomes, other factors such as rainfall, soil carbon and carbon 47 
inputs to soils (e.g., litterfall) appear to be equally or more important in explaining observed 48 
variability in Rs within biomes (Bond-Lamberty and Thomson, 2010a; Huang et al., 2020; Hursh 49 
et al., 2017; Raich et al., 2002). For example, in tropical biomes, Rs is often uncorrelated or only 50 
weakly correlated with temperature (Davidson et al., 2000; Rubio and Detto, 2017), possibly a 51 
consequence of the low intra-annual variability in temperatures experienced by these biomes 52 
when compared to temperate ecosystems.  53 
On the other hand, soil moisture has been shown to be an important driver of Rs in tropical 54 
systems (Adachi et al., 2009; Hashimoto et al., 2004; Rubio and Detto, 2017; Valentini et al., 55 
2008; Wood et al., 2013). However, the exact nature of this relationship is unclear and appears 56 
variable, with studies reporting positive (Hashimoto et al., 2004), negative (Epron et al., 2006) 57 
and parabolic relationships of Rs with soil moisture (Adachi et al., 2009; Gaumont-Guay et al., 58 
2006; Rubio and Detto, 2017; Sotta et al., 2004; Takahashi et al., 2011). Soil moisture can also 59 
mediate the effect of temperature on Rs, with low moisture typically constraining the response of 60 
Rs to temperature (Deng et al., 2012; Flanagan and Johnson, 2005; Hursh et al., 2017; Wen et al., 61 
2006; Wood et al., 2013), although the opposite – higher temperature sensitivity of Rs at low soil 62 
moisture - has also been observed (Guntiñas et al., 2013).  63 
As climate predictions for the future include changes in both temperature and rainfall patterns  64 
(IPCC, 2023), our ability to predict responses of the carbon cycle hinges upon a better 65 
understanding of the factors that influence soil carbon fluxes across different biomes. In 66 
particular, the limited numbers of empirical observations from tropical forests, which have 67

In this study, we characterise the response of soil respiration to changes in moisture availability 74 
and temperature in two seasonal wet evergreen forest plots in the Western Ghats, India.  These 75 
forests differ from other seasonal tropical wet forests in the length of the dry season (> 6 months) 76 
and in the intensity of rainfall they receive during the wet season (>2500 mm).  Specifically, our 77 
objectives were to 1) quantify and characterise inter-annual and seasonal variation of soil 78 
respiration and 2) examine the effects of temperature and moisture availability on soil respiration 79 
rates at annual, seasonal and instantaneous temporal scales. 80 
2. Methods 81 
2.1 Site description:   82 
We conducted the study at two long-term wet evergreen forest monitoring sites – Hosagadde 83 
(14.478° N, 74.760° E) and Mulagunda (14.471°N,74.693 °E) – located in the Uttara Kannada 84 
district in the state of Karnataka in the central Western Ghats, India. Both sites are at an 85 
elevation of ~550 m and have a long-term mean annual temperature of 24°C. The mean annual 86 
rainfall during the study period was 3730 mm and 4228 mm at Hosagadde and Mulagunda, 87 
respectively (University of East Anglia Climatic Research Unit; Harris et al., 2020). We assigned 88 
months with more than 100 mm rainfall as wet season (June – October), and months with <100 89 
mm rainfall as the dry season (November – May). Soils in the region have been classified as a 90 
mixture of Eutric Nitosols and Acrisols under the FAO system (FAO-UNESCO, 1988; 91 
Krishnaswamy et al., 2017; Rome, FAO, 1998). From our measurements, soils in Hosagadde 92 
have 28.1% clay, 63.5 % sand and a water holding capacity of 83.22%, and soils in Mulagunda 93 
have 21.6% clay, 70.41% sand and a water holding capacity of 77.41%. 94 
2.2 Field Measurements: 95 
We established 1ha permanent vegetation monitoring plots at Hosagadde and Mulagunda in 2013 96 
and 2014, respectively, based on the RAINFOR-GEM (Marthews et al., 2014) and CTFS 97 
protocols (Condit, 1998). All trees >10 cm girth at breast height (GBH) in plots were identified, 98 
spatially mapped, tagged and their GBH measured. Plots were re-censused annually. We 99 
additionally quantified soil respiration in plots following the protocols outlined in Marthews et 100 
al., (2014). In each plot, we established 25 polyvinyl carbonate (PVC) soil collars (10 cm height 101 
and 10.6 cm diameter) by inserting them 5-6 cm into the soil.  We used portable Infra-red gas 102 
analysers (IRGA) EGM-4 and EGM5 (PP Systems, USA) to measure instantaneous Rs (g CO2 m-103 
2.h-1). Soil respiration was measured fortnightly from January 2014 to August 2020 in 104 
Hosagadde, and from January 2015 till August 2020 in Mulagunda.  105

2.4 Statistical methods:  137 
We assessed the effects of temperature and moisture availability on Rs at multiple temporal 138 
scales – annual, seasonal and instantaneous.  At the annual scale, we used linear models to test 139 
the relationship between annual Rs and a) annual rainfall, b) mean annual temperature, c) 140 
number of rainy days, and d) number of days with heavy rainfall (>50mm). 141 
 142 
At the seasonal scale, we used a linear mixed effects model (LMMs) to test whether Rs values 143 
differed between wet and dry seasons, with season (wet, dry) included as a fixed factor and soil 144 
collar number nested within year within site as a random factor to account for inherent 145 
differences between soil collars within each site, and non-independence of measurements over 146 
time at each collar. We had 11-24 data points/collar/year/site for a total of 6886 observations.  147 
Finally, we used linear mixed effects models to test whether spatially averaged temperature and 148 
moisture levels could predict instantaneous Rs. For this, we used spatially averaged Rs (averaged 149 
over 25 collars) within each sampling period for each site as the response variable and spatially 150 
averaged temperature (as recorded by the IRGA) and a quadratic term with spatially averaged 151 
soil moisture and site ID as fixed factors. We used the calendar year as a random factor to 152 
account for inter-annual differences in soil respiration rates. We tested two candidate models: 153 
one which included an interaction term between temperature and soil moisture, and one without. 154 
In all, we had 13-24 sampling times/year/site and a total 276 spatially averaged Rs values. Rs 155 
values were square root transformed to meet model assumptions of linearity and 156 
homoscedasticity. 157 
We used the R packages glmmTMB for all models, DHARMa for model diagnostics, boxcox to 158 
inform us about suitable transformations to meet model assumptions, ggeffects, ggpubr and 159 
ggplot2 for data visualization and to plot model predictions, and dplyr for data cleaning and 160 
organisation. All analyses were carried out using R version 4.3.0 (R Core Team 2023). 161 
3. Results 162 
3.1. Rainfall, soil moisture and temperature seasonality: 163 
During the study period, annual rainfall in Hosagadde varied between 2800 - 5300 mm and in 164 
Mulagunda between 3700 - 7600 mm. Rainfall was very seasonal, occurring primarily during the 165 
south-west monsoon from June to October. Hosagadde received very heavy rains (>1000mm 166 
month-1) in July and August and Mulagunda between June and August. Outside of the monsoon, 167 
both sites received <100 mm rainfall month-1 (Fig 1A). Similar to rainfall variations, soil 168 
moisture levels were also strongly seasonal (Fig 1B). In both sites, they were lowest from 169 
February to April (average volumetric water content of 13.9% and 9.1%) and highest between 170 
July and October (35.6% and 30.2%). Mean annual temperatures during the study period at both 171 
sites were ~22°C. Soil temperatures (at 125mm depth) ranged between 17-26°C in Hosagadde 172 
and 19 to 30°C in Mulagunda (Fig 1C).  173

4. Discussion 202 
In this highly seasonal rainforest in the Western Ghats, rainfall was the dominant driver of soil 203 
respiration (Rs), influencing Rs at annual, seasonal and instantaneous time scales. Rs was lowest 204 
during periods of high soil moisture availability, i.e. during the wet-season in wetter years, and 205 
highest when soil moisture was lower, i.e. during the dry season and in drier years.  In contrast, 206

temperature was only of secondary importance, with warmer temperatures leading to higher rates 207 
of instantaneous CO2 efflux for any given soil moisture level. 208 
Instantaneous soil respiration showed a parabolic relationship with soil moisture availability, 209 
peaking at ~21% VWC and declining with both increases and decreases in soil moisture. Dry 210 
conditions can reduce Rs because of limited water availability for both microbial and root 211 
respiration, while high moisture availability can reduce rates of CO2 diffusion in waterlogged 212 
soils while also reducing microbial activity and root respiration as a result of soils becoming 213 
anoxic (Adachi et al., 2009; Cusack et al., 2019; Gaumont-Guay et al., 2006; Rubio and Detto, 214 
2017; Valentini et al., 2008; Wood et al., 2013). Similar parabolic relationships of Rs with soil 215 
moisture have also been reported from other tropical forests across the globe (Adachi et al., 216 
2009; Cusack et al., 2019; Koehler et al., 2009; Matson et al., 2017; Schwendenmann et al., 217 
2003; Sotta et al., 2004). The soil moisture at peak respiration at our sites is comparable with 218 
reported values from Southeast Asian forests (~21%) (Adachi et al., 2009; Takahashi et al., 219 
2011), but lower than those from the Neotropics (30-40%; Sotta et al., 2004; Gaumont-Guay et 220 
al., 2006; Rubio and Detto, 2017; Wood et al., 2013), potentially a consequence of differences in 221 
soil texture across these sites. Soil wa ter content for optimal R s  has previously be en shown to be 222 
correlated with soil text ure and clay content (Balogh et al. , 201 1; Cabl e et al ., 20 08) .  223 
Patterns of seasonal Rs in our sites are consistent with earlier results from neo-tropical forests 224 
that report lower Rs in wet seasons relative to dry seasons in high rainfall sites (>4000 mm 225 
MAP). In contrast, studies from drier sites report higher wet-season Rs relative to dry-season Rs 226 
(Cusack et al., 2019; Fernandes et al., 2002; Hashimoto et al., 2004; Koehler et al., 2009; Matson 227 
et al., 2017; Rubio and Detto, 2017; Schwendenmann et al., 2003; Sotta et al., 2006, 2004). Our 228 
results are also in accordance with experimental dry-down studies that report increases in Rs 229 
following rainfall reductions in wet sites, and decreases in Rs with drought in drier sites 230 
(Cleveland et al., 2010; van Straaten et al., 2011; Wood et al., 2013). Thus, at a global scale, the 231 
nature of the relationship between seasonal Rs and rainfall is likely determined by where sites lie 232 
along the dry-wet gradient. 233 
In both our study sites, annual soil respiration was negatively correlated with the frequency of 234 
high rainfall events (>50mm day-1), but was not correlated with the frequency of rainy days, 235 
indicating that rainfall intensity is a key factor in limiting soil respiration in these forests. Not 236 
only do these sites receive extremely high rainfall, most of this rainfall occurs during the five 237 
monsoon months, with the wettest months receiving >1000mm rainfall month-1, while the rest of 238 
the year is mostly dry. Inhibition of soil respiration following extreme rain events has also been 239 
previously reported from other Asian monsoonal forests (Yu et al., 2021), and suggest that future 240 
increases in the frequency and intensity of extreme rain events are likely to significantly impact 241 
annual soil CO2 effluxes in these forests. Temperature, on the other hand, did not appear to be a 242 
strong driver of annual soil respiration rates in our study site, as has also been reported from 243

invariant, with a range of less than 10 degrees in both of our sites.  245 
Despite our two study sites being in close proximity to one another, soil CO2 efflux rates differed 246 
substantially between the two sites. Mean annual Rs ranged from 8.44 – 12.05 Mg.ha-1.yr-1 247 
(mean = 9.85 Mg.ha-1.yr-1) at Mulagunda, and from 10.9 – 20.2 Mg.ha-1.yr-1 (mean = 14.58 248 
Mg.ha-1.yr-1) at Hosagadde, as compared to the global mean of ~13.1 Mg.ha-1.yr-1 reported for 249 
tropical broadleaved forests (Raich et al., 2002; Warner et al., 2019). The higher rainfall levels 250 
experienced in Mulgunda (Fig 1a) is a potential contributor to the lower fluxes at this site.  251 
However, even for the same level of soil moisture, instantaneous Rs tended to be lower in 252 
Mulgunda compared to Hosagadde (Fig 2d). While the reasons underlying these differences are 253 
currently unclear, it is likely that other factors including edaphic properties – which have been 254 
shown to exert significant controls on soil respiration rates, potentially more so than climatic 255 
factors (Haaf et al., 2021), soil nutrient availability, litter production and availability of substrate 256 
for microbes also contribute to the observed differences (Davidson et al., 2002; Yao et al., 2021). 257 
 258 
Our study, the first to detail soil respiration at multiple temporal scales from a highly seasonal 259 
rainforest in the Western Ghats of India contributes to the growing body of literature on controls 260 
of soil respiration across the tropics. The combination of very high rainfall and extreme 261 
seasonality in these sites places them in a unique climatic niche globally, and adds valuable 262 
insights into our broader understanding of the potential relationships between rainfall regimes 263 
and soil respiration. Our key finding that both high annual rainfall and extremely rainy days 264 
suppress soil respiration indicates that future changes in the climatology of the monsoon in this 265 
region is likely to significantly influence carbon effluxes from these system in the future. 266 
Experimental studies that manipulate the intensity and frequency of heavy rainfall events can 267 
help to better quantify these patterns and thereby better parameterize earth system models. 268 
Further, separation of autotrophic and heterotrophic components of respiration can allow us to 269 
quantify the sensitivity of each component and better understand the biology underlying carbon 270 
fluxes in this ecosystem. 271
