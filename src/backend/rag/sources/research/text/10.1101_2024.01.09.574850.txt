Survey of the wheat mycobiome associated with leaf blotch and head blight diseases in Sweden

Increasing the durability of crop resistance to plant pathogens has become a major concern in plant pathology. There are 32 diseases of agronomical importance in wheat, which makes disease management in this crop of worldwide importance extremely challenging. Two important groups of wheat pathogens driving the consumption of chemical fungicides in Sweden and worldwide are those causing head blight diseases and leaf blotch diseases. Swedish fields are regularly infested with head blight diseases typically driven by a complex of Fusarium and non-fusarium taxa including F. graminearum, F. culmorum, while leaf blotches are typically caused by Zymoseptoria tritici, Pyrenophora tritici-repentis, and Parastagonospora nodorum. In this work, we present a survey of the larger diversity of taxa associated with head blight and leaf blotch diseases in Sweden, with the primary aim to assess the usefulness of such an approach to develop new understanding and tools for disease diagnostics and management sensu lato. Here, we analyze nearly a thousand isolates collected from wheat fields across eight counties in Sweden and report the identification of pathogenic and non-pathogenic taxa as well as candidate biological control agents associated with head blight and leaf blotch diseases. We argue that such surveys represent an important tool for improving disease diagnostics, disease forecasting, and integrated management in wheat.

Introduction 39 
Disease identification in the field can be very challenging , considering the complexity and the 40 
convergence of symptoms caused by different pathogen species. For diseases typically caused by a 41 
complex such as Fusarium Head Blight (FHB), several fungal species can be implicated. For instance, 42 
there are 17 Fusarium sp. and non -Fusarium species implicated in FHB. Of these, F. graminearum 43 
and F. culmorum are often presented as the most important drivers of the disease, with F. graminearum 44 
often being the most predominant species (Karlsson et al., 2021). The composition of the complex 45 
can significantly vary among countries depending on the varieties grown in the field, agricultural 46 
practices, or climate (Karlsson et al., 2021). For instance, a field monitoring study conducted by Xu 47 
et al. (2005) in four European countries (Hungary, Ireland, Italy, and the UK), it was shown that FHB 48 
was frequently associated with F. graminearum and F. poae in Italy and Hungary  but not in Ireland 49 
and the UK , while F. culmorum  was rarely detected except in Ireland  (Xu et al., 2005). This 50 
demonstrates the importance of regular ly monitoring FHB for the correct identification of the causal 51 
agents and proper assessing the efficiency of management strategies.  52 
 53 
Other prominent examples of phenotypic complexity are leaf blotch diseases caused by Zymoseptoria 54 
tritici, Pyrenophora tritici-repentis, and Parastagonospora nodorum. All pathogens cause convergent 55 
disease symptoms that can be confounded with each other. Actually, the three pathogen species were 56 
historically confounded as one species, then Z. tritici and P. nodorum were referred to as the Septoria 57 
blotch complex or Septoria complex due to frequent co-occurrence of the two species and the similarity 58 
of symptoms. From a resistance breeding perspective, the distinction between those three species is 59 
crucial since it was shown that the host resistance is genetically different (i.e. , requires different 60 
resistance genes, resistance mechanisms, and breeding strategies) (Brown et al., 2015). This further 61 
demonstrates the importance of monitoring and precise disease diagnostics as a tool for disease 62 
management. Furthermore, leaf blotch diseases in wheat are still largely controlled with fungicides, 63 
leading to the rapid development of fungicide resistance in pathogen populations across Europe  64 
(Pereira et al., 2020; Justesen et al., 2021; Jørgensen et al., 2021, Jørgensen et al., 2022) Thus, 65 
surveys are essential for the management of fungicide resistance and further highlight the importance 66 
of continuous isolation and characterization of pathogen populations from the field.  67

One major challenge for disease diagnosis in wheat is the recurrent emergence of hypervirulent 69 
pathogen races, with one prominent example being the stem UG99 from the wheat pathogen Puccinia 70 
graminis f. sp. tritici, that was responsible for a global pandemic of stem rust in wheat  (Singh et al., 71 
2011). From a phenotypic perspective, it is impossible to distinguish the stem rust symptoms caused 72 
by the UG99 race from any other race, thus highlighting the importance of molecular race 73 
characterization for proper monitoring and resistance breeding (Patpour et al., 2022). A similar 74 
challenge is imposed by Blumeria graminis f.sp. triticale (B.g. triticale), a new wheat pathogen that 75 
emerged from the hybridization of wheat powdery mildew (B.g. tritici) and rye powdery mildew (B.g. 76 
secalis) (Menardo et al., 2016). B.g. triticale is a new pathogen with an expanded host range that can 77 
not only infect the Triticale crop but also wheat, causing symptoms that are indistinguishable from the 78 
native wheat powdery mildew species B.g. tritici  (Menardo et al., 2016) These examples further 79 
demonstrate the importance of monitoring and the molecular identification of the causal agent as a tool 80 
for the surveillance of emerging and re-emerging diseases.  81 
 82 
The wheat mycobiome has been shown to be an important source of beneficial fungal endophytes with 83 
promising biological control properties. Such antagonists often occur in the same ecological 84 
niches/tissues as their ‘target’ pathogen species and have thus been isolated from various organs, 85 
including roots, leaves, seeds, or ears  (Huang & Pang, 2017; Yue et al., 2018; Rojas et al., 2020). 86 
Considering the genetic diversity of the endophytic taxa inhabiting wheat, only a fraction of the wheat 87 
mycobiome has been characterized for its biological control properties. Such work is mainly restricted 88 
by the tedious initial isolation and molecular characterization of hundreds (if not thousands) of fungal 89 
taxa. We, therefore, argue that surveys for diseases could be coupled with surveys for biological control 90 
agents, taking advantage of the availability of host tissues where both pathogens and potential 91 
antagonists can be isolated. In this study, we aimed to provide an experimental proof-of-concept for 92 
the possible uses of disease sampling and monitoring campaigns for constructing a wheat mycobiome 93 
library that can be used  to understand and monitor wheat head blight and leaf blotch diseases in 94 
Sweden. In particular, we assess if such a library can inform on s hifts in pathogen populations and 95 
possibly lead to the identification  of potential emerging diseases  and candidate biological control 96 
agents. 97

Materials and methods 99 
Sample collection and handling 100 
A survey of wheat diseases was conducted in collaboration with the plant protection extension services 101 
of the Swedish Board of Agriculture. In 2020, spikes were collected from spring wheat varieties 102 
cultivated in Sweden and grown in a small propagation plot north of Stockholm. The plot consisted of 103 
the six Spring wheat varieties: Alderon, Dacke, Dala, Happy, Rohan, Stanley ; two spring wheat 104 
genotypes used as scientific standards: Bobwhite and Fielder; and one triticale genotype. In 2021 and 105 
2023, spike and leaf samples were collected by field pathologists from the plant protection extension 106 
services from eight counties across Sweden: Norrbotten, Uppsala, Stockholm, Södermanlands, 107 
Östergötland, Västra Götaland , Gottland, and Skåna (Figure 1 A ). Samples were typically shipped  108 
within 24 hours in glycine bags and immediately processed or stored at 4°C in dry storage until further 109 
processing.  110 
 111 
Media preparation for isolation and long-term conservation 112 
Two different media were used for pathogen isolation, purification, and long -term storage. Potato 113 
dextrose agar (PDA) was prepared from dehydrated pre -mixed media from MP Biomedicals (USA) 114 
according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Yeast malt sucrose (YMS) agar medium was prepared by 115 
mixing solve 4 g yeast extract, 4 g malt extract, and 4 g sucrose, and 16 g agar in 1000 ml milliQ water, 116 
then autoclaving for 5 min at 121°C. For mid-term storage, isolates were kept as slow-growing cultures 117 
on half-strength PDA or YMS agar in small glass vials and stored in the dark at 4°C. For long -term 118 
storage, three to four ca, 5mm mycelial plugs were cut from a growing colon, placed in a 2ml cryotube 119 
containing 30% glycerol, and stored at -80°C. For Z. tritici, long-term storage vials were prepared from 120 
liquid cultures as previously described by Fagundes and collaborators (Fagundes et al., 2020). 121

DNA extraction and genotyping by fragment sequencing 143 
A modified cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) based DNA extraction method  was used to 144 
extract DNA of fungal isolates . Briefly, mycelium plugs were used to inoculate a liquid medium 145 
containing malt extracts and vitamins (MEV). Cultures were incubated with ca. 150rpm shaking in the 146 
dark at 25℃ for two weeks. Then, mycelium was harvested, homogenized with a CTAB buffer (3% 147 
CTAB; 150 mM TRIS-HCl; 2.6 M NaCl; 20 mM EDTA), and incubated at 65℃ for 30 min inside a 148 
water bath. The samples were centrifuged at 10’000 g for 10 min and the supernatant was transferred 149 
to a new tube  supplemented with an equal volume of chloroform . After a second centrifugation at 150 
10’000 g for 10 min , the supernatant was transferred to a new tube , an equal amount of isopropanol 151 
was added, and the mix was incubated at -20℃ for a few minutes. A final centrifugation at 10’000 g 152 
for 10 min resulted in the deposition of a DNA pellet, which was washed using 70% ethanol  and 153 
resuspended in TE buffer. In addition, another DNA extraction method was used to extract the DNA 154 
of the fungal isolates. First, mycelium plugs from the isolates were cultured in falcon tubes containing 155 
10 ml of YMS liquid medium and incubated for 7 -15 days at 150 rpm and 25 °C in dark. Then, the 156 
mycelium was harvested, freeze-dried, and subjected to genomic DNA extraction using the Nucleo 157 
Mag Plant kit (Macherey-Nagel GmbH &Co. KG, Germany) in the automated nucleic acid extractor 158 
Maelstrom 4800 (TANBead Taiwan Advanced Nanotech Inc., Taiwan). 159

Identification of pathogen species was carried out by Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) of the genomic 161 
DNA followed by amplicon sequencing of conserved nuclear locus, internal transcribed spacer (ITS) 162 
rDNA. For ITS PCR, primer pair ITS1 (5’- TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCGG-3’) and ITS4  (5- 163 
TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3’) were used for genotyping (White T.J., 1990). PCR amplification 164 
was followed by PCR clean -up using exonuclease  kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific)  according to the 165 
manufacturer’s instructions. Amplicons were subsequently sent for fragment sequencing at Macrogen 166 
Europe (The Netherlands, https://www.macrogen-europe.com/). DNA sequences were manually 167 
curated and analyzed for sequence homology to fungal sequences deposited at the National Center for 168 
Biotechnology Information  (NCBI) using the BLASTn search tool. Isolates used for leaf infection 169 
assays were also tested with F. graminearum  marker GOFW (5’-170 
ACCTCTGTTGTTCTTCCAGACGG-3’) GORV (5’- CTGGTCAGTATTAACCGTGTGTG-3’) (de 171 
Biazio et al., 2008), and for F. culmorum with Fc03 (5’-TTCTTGCTAGGGTTGAGGATG-3’) Fc02 172 
(5’-GACCTTGACTTTGAGCTTCTTG-3’) (Astrid Bauer and Seigner, 2015). 173

Plant cultivation and infection assays 175 
Wheat plants were grown in the phytotron (controlled climate chambers) for three to four weeks at 176 
20℃, 70% relative humidity and 300µMOL light intensity.  The inoculum was composed of isolate 177 
HK48 (Fusarium graminearum), HK50 ( Fusarium culmorum). The isolates were incubated for two 178 
weeks at 25℃ and constant darkness on OMA. The conidia were scraped from the colony surface and 179 
resuspended in sterile water. The inoculum cell density was adjusted to 250 spores per µl using water. 180 
Leaf inoculation was based on a protocol originally developed at the Leibniz Institute of Plant Genetics 181 
and Crop Plant Research  and adapted to our conditions . Three- to four-weeks-old wheat leaves were 182 
randomly selected and cut into 4 cm length s. A hole was punctured in the middle of the leaf segment 183 
and inoculated with a 30 µl spore suspension. Three biological replicates consisting of each of two 184 
technical replicates were i noculated with either F. graminearum or F. culmorum. A total of 360 leaf 185 
segments were placed in water agar and incubated in phytotron for four days. The necrosis was 186 
observed in each of the 360 leaf segments and scored visually. 187

Isolate pictures were analyzed using the open-source Fiji from ImageJ  (Schindelin et al., 2012). To 190 
minimize noise from the background, the region of interest (ROI) was defined manually with the circle 191 
tool and added to the ROI manager (“Analyze/Tools/ROI manager/Add” and “Edit/Clear outside”). 192 
Once the ROI was defined, general color data was extracted (“Analyzed/color histogram”). After this 193 
first step, the image was split into three different channels (blue, green, and red), and the different 194 
features of interest were extracted for each channel (“ROI manager/Measure”). To proceed with texture 195 
analysis of each channel image, we analyze d the image with the GLCM texture macro, defining the 196 
size of the step in pixels as 1 and the direction of the step as 0 degrees (“Plugins/GLCM texture”). 197 
Finally, the image type  was changed to 32-bit (“Image/Type/32-bit”) to perform the last step of the 198 
analysis. We selected SurfCharJ1q macro (“Plugins/ SurfCharJ1q”) to analyze roughness features. At 199 
the end of the analysis, each sample had 90 different variables to be studied. 200 
 201 
Statistical analysis 202 
All statistical analyses were performed with R (version 4.2.2.) in Rstudio environment (R Core Team, 203 
2021). The packages used for the analys es were: caret, cluster, corrplot, datawizard, factoextra, 204 
faraway, readxl,  and stats. Data collected from ImageJ was normalized throughout the Z-scoring 205 
transformation. After data transformation , a linear model was adapted to analyze the putative 206 
correlation among the studied variables. Variables without changes among samples or with a 207 
correlation coefficient above 0.9 were removed. After this first filtering  step, we performed a second 208 
selection, deleting variables with the highest correlation coefficients and variance inflation factors 209 
(VIF) values. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed with the filtered and normalized 210 
data. To perform clustering analysis, agglomerative nesting (AGNES) and divisive analysis (DIANA) 211 
algorithms were applied for hierarchical clustering over our filtered data. Finally, kmeans and medoids 212 
analysis was performed with a predefined number of clusters of 2. Kmeans analysis was performed 213 
with Hartigan and Wong algorithm and Euclidean distance was selected for medoids analysis. The 214 
aggregation level of each cluster was measured throughout their silhouette value. 215

Results 218 
Overall diversity of the wheat mycobiome library 219 
In this study, hundreds of colonies were isolated from spikes and leaves collected over three field 220 
campaigns in eight counties across Sweden ( Figure 1A). Of these, several isolates did not make it to 221 
the final set due to practical issues with growth, bacterial contaminations, failure of the genotyping at 222 
the PCR or the fragment-sequencing step. Still, we were able to recover a final set of 940 successfully 223 
genotyped samples which was the basis for the following analyzes. First, we concatenated all the 224 
genotypic data and evaluated the overall diversity of the resulting fungal collection  in terms of genus 225 
diversity (Figure 1B ). Here , it is important to read the following primarily in terms of qualitative 226 
diversity, considering the inherent bias due to the isolation method, and where abundance is primarily 227 
meant in terms of frequency of isolation. We were able to recover taxa from 14 different genera with 228 
a minimum of two representative (i.e. independently recovered isolates). Of these, the most abundant 229 
genera included Fusarium sp., the ubiquitous Alternaria sp., and to a lesser extent Zymoseptoria sp., 230 
Pyrenophora sp., Microdochium sp., and Parastagonospora sp., which is consistent with the fact that 231 
the sampling was mainly targeted to FHB, Leaf Blotch diseases of wheat. We therefore conclude that 232 
the resulting isolate library is relevant to the diseases covered by the survey.  233 
 234 
We then assessed the species diversity of the two most abundant taxa , namely, Fusarium sp. and 235 
Alternaria sp (Figure 1C, 1D). We found that the most abundant Alternaria species were A. infectoria, 236 
and A. alternata, which are commonly associated with head diseases such as the wheat black point , 237 
and also to mycotoxin production (DeMers M., 2022). (Figure 1C). For Fusarium sp., we were able 238 
to recover a diversity of taxa typically associated with FHB in wheat, with the most common species 239 
including F. culmorum, F. graminearum, F. poae, and F. avenaceum (Figure 1D). We conclude that 240 
the collected data indicate a large diversity of taxa typically implicated in head diseases of wheat 241 
present in Swedish wheat fields, representing a potential risk for disease outbreaks. 242

agents as well as potential antagonists potentially corresponding to candidate biological control agents. 245

niches and may thus be co -isolated from the same tissues. The library contained 23 different fungal 247

Inventory of pathogenic and endophytic isolates associated with Leaf Blotch in Sweden. 258 
For the leaf mycobiome associated with Leaf Blotch diseases, we recovered isolates from typical blotch 259 
symptoms as well as from symptomless tissues. We deduced that symptomless tissues may be the 260 
habitat for endophytic fungi that could be antagonistic to the pathogenic taxa  or harbor  pathogens 261

Overall, Alternaria species dominated the infection, which is consistent with the fact that they are 264 
known to be ubiquitously found as weak pathogens or saprophytes on different plant tissue ( Figure 265 
2A). Alternaria sp. apart, the most frequently isolated species were Zymoseptoria tritici, Pyrenophora 266 
tritici-repentis, and Parastagonospora nodorum, all of which being prominent causal agents of blotch 267 
diseases in wheat. We here conclude that the collection isolated from leave does represent the diseases 268

We also tested the capacity of F. culmorum, F. graminearum to cause blotch-like symptoms on wheat 281 
leaves. To do so, we performed point inoculation of leaf segments harvested at the seedling stage and 282 
visually assessed the development of necrotic lesions (see Methods). We inoculated 30 commercial 283 
spring wheat cultivars commonly grown in Sweden ( Figure 2 D, 2E ). We found that F. culmorum 284 
caused necrotic lesions on at least 5 out of the 30 commercial cultivars tested  (Figure 2D), while F. 285 
graminearum caused necrotic lesions on 15 commercial cultivars (Figure 2 E). These results 286 
substantiate the hypothesis that F. graminearum and F. culmorum can potentially cause leaf blotch -287 
like symptoms in Swedish fields on several of the commonly grown spring wheat varieties. 288

Inventory of pathogenic and endophytic isolates associated with FHB in Sweden 290 
For the spike mycobiome, we recovered isolates from seeds as well as from glumes and searched for 291 
both pathogenic and non-pathogenic taxa. Our reasoning was that glumes may offer a secondary habitat 292 
for wheat ear pathogens or potential antagonist s competing for the same ecological niche in the seed 293 
vicinity. In contrast to the leaf mycobiome, Fusarium species dominated over Alternaria sp., including 294 
the two most important causal agents of FHB, namely F. culmorum and F. graminearum (Figure 3A). 295 
In addition, several typical members of the FHB complex were isolated, including F. poae , F. 296 
avenaceum, F. equiseti, and M. nivale. We then investigated the differences in the qualitative diversity 297 
between the taxa recovered from seeds vs. glumes (Figure 3B, 3C). Interestingly, the less pathogenic 298 
taxa F. poae  and F. avenaceum  were mainly  isolated from seeds  (Figure 3B ), while the more 299 
pathogenic taxa F. graminearum and F. culmorum were isolated from both seed compartments but 300 
dominated in the glumes ( Figure 3C). This data suggests  that Fusarium species implicated in FHB 301 
fully occupy the seed habitat, with the glumes possibly acting as a secondary niche where pathogenic 302 
taxa seem to outcompete other fungal taxa, such as Alternaria sp which has been isolated ubiquitously 303 
from all tissues analyzed in this study except glumes. 304

county, Figure 1A). Interestingly, we mostly recovered M. nivale from the leaf blotch symptoms and 323 
F. avenaceum and M. nivale from the FHB-infected ears (Figure 3G). These results suggest that in 324 
these northern latitudes, blotch symptoms are probably cased by M. nivale, which is the common agent 325 
of the Snow Mold  disease of wheat, while FHB is possibly caused by the weaker pathogens F. 326 
avenaceum and M. nivale. We conclude that such observations demonstrate the usefulness of such 327 
surveys to shed light on possible changes in the disease landscape and alert on the possible re -328 
emergence of diseases. 329

Discussion 372 
Wheat pathogens occupy a variety of tissues and niches 373 
In this study, we recovered typical wheat pathogens implicated in head blight and leaf blotch diseases 374 
from a variety of tissues, including seeds, glumes, symptomatic leaf tissues, and asymptomatic leaves. 375 
We had cases where Fusarium taxa typically implicated in FHB were isolated from blotch symptoms 376 
in the leaves and other cases where typical blotch pathogens such as P. tritici-repentis, and P. nodorum 377 
were isolated from wheat ears. Furthermore, typical leaf pathogens were also isolated as silent 378 
endophytes from asymptomatic tissues. Fusarium species are well known as ubiquitous plant 379 
colonizers that can survive in a variety of plant tissues and also in the soil  (Karlsson et al., 2021). In 380 
disease complex es such as FHB , where 17 different Fusarium and non -Fusarium taxa can be 381 
implicated, the role of such secondary niches is poorly understood  (Karlsson et al., 2021). From a 382 
population perspective, the capacity of pathogens to survive in tissues or organs that are not their 383 
primary habitat may increase the fitness of the pathogen population in the field by maintaining living 384 
inoculum, throughout the field seasons and particularly when conditions are unfavorable. This was, for 385 
instance, observed in the case of Z. tritici, where it was shown that avirulent isolates can still survive 386 
and mate on resistant wheat hosts as symptomless endophytes or epiphytes (Fones et al., 2023), thus 387 
contributing to pathogen fitness and adaptation through the maintenance of genetic diversity in the 388 
field population. It was also shown that Z. tritici can induce systemic responses leading to whole plant 389 
metabolic changes leading to increased susceptibility to secondary infection with a non -adapted 390 
pathogen (Seybold et al., 2020). This is major because such mechanisms could also occur among 391 
different isolates of the same pathogen population or different species of a disease complex, thus 392 
providing a possible explanation for the functional relevance of secondary niches and the ubiquitous 393 
nature of the taxa implicated in FHB. 394

The wheat mycobiome as a source for candidate biological control agents 396 
Microbial biological control agents are considered an important tool to reduce chemical inputs in crop 397 
disease management. A variety of microbial agents with antagonistic properties against plant pathogens 398 
has been described, and variety of mode of action underlying such effect has been reported including 399 
antibiosis, parasitism, niche competition, or resistance priming in the host  (Köhl et al., 2019). 400 
Microbial biological control agents that correspond to naturally occurring competitors are commonly 401 
found in the same ecological niches as their target pests. In this study, several candidate biological 402

Wheat disease diagnostics and its importance for management 413 
The beneficial vs. pathogenic lifestyle of a plant colonizing fungus can be determined mainly by the 414 
host and the ecological niches it occupies. For instance, the biological control fungus Clonostachys 415 
rosea, a naturally occurring root endophyte, has been shown to be effective against several foliar plant 416 
pathogens. However, C. rosea has also been reported as the causal agent of root  rot, crown rot, and  417 
wilt-type diseases in garlic, faba bean, avocado, strawberry, sugar beet, and medicinal plants such as 418 
Angelica sinensis and Astragalus mongholicus (Ma et al., 2020; Qi et al., 2022; Diaz et al., 2022; 419 
Afshari et al., 2017; Coyotl-Pérez et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2022; Haque and Parvin, 2020, 420 
Farhaoui et al., 2023). Similarly, E. nigrum has been described as a potent biological control agent 421 
against wheat stripe rust . However, E. nigrum is also the causal agent of the brown spot disease  in 422 
Solanaceae (Jin et al., 2023). These reports highlight the importance of continuous monitoring of field 423 
populations and careful, systematic identification of the causal agents for identifying potential lifestyle 424 
shifts in microbial populations.  425 
 426 
Shifts from a beneficial endophytic lifestyle to pathogenicity through host switch, niche displacement, 427 
and changes in the microbiome  have been well documented for several prominent diseases such as 428 
Diplodia sapinea, the causal agent of diplodia tip blight in Scots pine, and Sarocladium oryzae the 429 
causal agent of rice sheet rot  (Blumenstein et al., 2022; Peeters et al., 2021). Therefore, the 430 
designation of beneficial endophyte/ candidate biological control agents must be taken within the 431 
context of the actual contribution of the species to the wheat mycobiome, meaning that both disease-432 
suppressing antagonistic and disease-promoting synergistic properties must be tested. This is directly 433

A basis for targeted monitoring of potentially emerging wheat diseases 448 
The emergence of new diseases leading to global epidemics is relatively common in wheat, with stem 449 
rust and wheat blast being two prominent examples. Wheat has also been the host for the emergence 450 
of two new pathogens that did not existence before namely triticale powdery mildew and wheat blast. 451 
Arguably, the large reservoir of wheat pathogens present in the agro-ecosystem is a challenge requiring 452 
continuous monitoring for early detection of potentially new, emerging diseases. In this context, two 453 
taxa identified in our survey are of particular interest: N. oryzae, and S. vesicarium (Table 1B). N. 454 
oryzae is an ascomycete fungal pathogen and the causal agent of p anicle branch rot disease on rice 455 
(Oryza sativa ) (Liu et al., 2021) In 2016, N. oryzae was reported to cause black lesions on the 456 
hypocotyls of wheat seedlings in Kazakhstan, suggesting that some isolates of N. oryzae  (a rice 457 
pathogen) may have adapted to be pathogens on wheat, which is reminiscent of the emergence of wheat 458 
blast (Eken et al., 2016). Smilarly, S. vesicarium has been reported to cause leaf spot s on wheat in 459 
Egypt (Mohamed et al., 2022), and to our knowledge, these pathogens have never been described in 460 
Sweden before. We conclude that such results further highlight the relevance of this work since we can 461 
now provide a rational basis for risk assessment and targeted disease monitoring of critical pathogens 462 
in Swedish wheat fields. 463
